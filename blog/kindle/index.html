my kindle thinks i died in 2022

141 hours, 3,705 sessions, a 905-day flatline, and a resurrection: what seven years of kindle telemetry actually shows

amazon will hand you everything it knows about your kindle if you ask. i asked. it knows a lot: 410 csv files covering every reading session since 2019, every page flip, every dictionary lookup, every time i changed a font setting, every lockscreen ad it showed me, and, my favorite, every time the e-reader woke up and immediately went back to sleep.

i pointed seven analysis agents at the pile expecting a list of books. what came back was a three-act story told entirely in timestamps.

the flatline

reading minutes per month, aug 2019 → aug 2026. the shaded band is 905 consecutive days in which i did not open a book once.

act one: 2019, new kindle, 32 hours of reading, quantum physics and mountaineering disasters. act two: the slow fade. 25 hours in 2020, 19 in 2021, 90 minutes total in 2022. then may 4, 2022: the last session. after that, nothing. not a short gap. 905 days. all of 2023: zero minutes. as far as the telemetry is concerned i was deceased.

the library kept logging purchases through the silence, and the purchases explain it:

reading stopped three months before 1L orientation and stayed stopped through law school and the startup that followed it. act three: a new kindle arrives april 7, 2026, at the start of a summer off, and within 24 hours i'm reading again. the four months since have produced 59 hours, more than any full year in the dataset.

the habit became structurally unbreakable

probability of reading tomorrow, given the current streak length. left: the 2019–2022 era. right: 2026. the red bars are certainties.

this is my favorite result in the whole export. in the first era, 57% of reading streaks died at day one; the chance a one-day streak survived to day two was 44%, and no streak ever passed 12 days. in 2026, once a streak reaches day 2, the observed probability that i read the next day is 100% through day 14 (70 of 70 day-observations continued). the only risky day is day 1. same brain, categorically different process: the longest streak is now 39 days, and i've read on 113 of the 124 days since the new kindle arrived.

the daily dose is boring in the best way: ~41 minutes per day in the first 30 days, ~41 in the last 30. what changed is the shape. median session grew from 15 seconds to 78, on fewer, longer sittings. the habit isn't growing; it consolidated.

midnight is my biggest reading hour

reading minutes by hour of day, in true local time (the jul–aug sessions are converted to sf/tokyo/taipei clocks per the phone's own timezone stamps). red bars are midnight through 4 a.m.

an honesty note first: i've been in asia since mid-july, and in the raw utc data that trip masquerades as a sudden 3 a.m. reading habit, then as me becoming a morning person. neither happened. corrected to where i actually was, my evening anchor is identical on both sides of the planet (center of mass ~7:30 p.m. local), and the night-owl finding survives cleanly: 26% of every minute i've ever read, about 37 hours, happened between midnight and 4 a.m. local time, with midnight itself the single biggest hour (924 minutes).

it's not insomnia, it's momentum: 86% of late-night sessions are continuations of an evening session that didn't stop. one detail worth noting: on the asia leg, the after-midnight share collapsed to 2%. jet lag did what intention never has.

the median session is 35 seconds

left: share of sessions by length. right: share of actual reading time. 86% of sessions are under five minutes; the 5–15 minute sittings carry the most minutes.

70% of all sessions are under two minutes, which initially reads as a destroyed attention span. it isn't, quite: 79% of those micro-sessions are warm-ups, followed by more reading within half an hour. a typical reading day is one main sitting that carries 74% of the day's minutes, plus a handful of short attempts around it.

median session length followed a u-curve: 147 seconds in 2019, down to 12 seconds in early 2026, back to 78 in the last 30 days. the longest continuous sitting of the entire seven years happened three weeks ago: 190 minutes of shackleton's endurance in a tokyo hotel.

what i actually read: three epochs

the content tells the same story as the timestamps, so the agents named my reading eras like geological periods:

  • the optimization eon (2019–2022), 4,666 min. gladwell, sinek, collins, "built to last", a gates biography, self-improvement, and quantum physics primers after midnight. fiction: 2.9%.
  • the founder flicker (2024–25), 268 min. five weeks of saas playbook, lean startup, and venture deals on a phone, then 14 minutes in all of 2025.
  • the survival & feeling supereon (2026), 3,552 min. the first week is attachment and trauma psychology (attached, the body keeps the score); then it finishes the jordan peterson book abandoned 1,434 days earlier, and moves through glass castle, shackleton, horror novels, caro. fiction jumps to 19.5%. four months out-read the best full year of the first era.

one theme survives every era: people enduring extreme circumstances. into thin air, on desperate ground, endurance, the glass castle: about 24 hours lifetime, the most durable interest in the record. physics did not survive; it fell from 524 minutes to 16 to 6 across the eras, and the book i've spent the most time on ever, 7.3 hours of what is real?, a book about the unfinished quest for the meaning of quantum physics, remains unfinished.

the "not interested" list is its own dataset: atomic habits, sapiens (rejected twice), outliers, blink, zero to one, infinite jest. per the recommendation logs, amazon's response to someone who rejected two gladwell books was to recommend more gladwell.

things the telemetry would rather i didn't know

  • buying the ad-supported kindle saved $20 and has cost 432 minutes of lockscreen ads (572 impressions). that's an effective wage of $2.78/hour for watching ads, below every us minimum wage. i have clicked exactly one ad, ever.
  • blended cost of my library: $20.61 per hour actually read. 79% of the dollars sit in books with under ten minutes of reading. best value: a $1 thriller i read for 3.2 hours ($0.31/hr). worst: influence, $22 for 4.2 minutes ($318/hr), and i own two editions of it.
  • amazon doesn't store search text, only lengths. but at 2:26 a.m. on jul 18, a 25-character search, the exact length of "the count of monte cristo", opened that book's page, and by the afternoon one of only two audible listens in seven years was logged: the count of monte cristo.
  • 276 of my 282 logged settings changes are toggling one setting: the reading-progress display, cycled between "time left in chapter", "time left in book", and "page number", 138 times in may 2026 alone. current answer: "time left in book".
  • on the phone, reading gets interrupted every 83 seconds (757 "app backgrounded" events against 17.6 hours of reading). the e-reader just idles to sleep every 12.5 minutes. that ratio is the entire phone-vs-e-reader argument in one number.
  • kindle points: 446 earned lifetime, 254 silently expired, 162 remaining. more points have expired than currently exist.
  • font settings across seven years and two devices: bookerly, size 4, justified. two recorded experiments with size 5, both reverted within minutes.
  • my "kindle" data export is 38% fire tv stick telemetry. the stick was used 5 days in 8 months, its screensaver out-ran netflix 118 minutes to 92, and on all 5 tv days my reading minutes were exactly zero.
  • one of the e-reader's internal settings is named, in production telemetry shipped to millions of devices, "prower saver".

what i take from it

the whole dataset reduces to one finding: my reading life is not a function of discipline, intention, or how much i claim to love books. it's a function of whether a dedicated e-ink device is physically near my bed, and whether something with the demands of law school is in session. when the device was there, i read 78 hours, then 59 more and counting, on a streak process that hasn't failed past day two. when it wasn't, eighteen months of phone-only reading produced four hours.

the $110 device outperformed the self-image by an order of magnitude. that seems worth knowing.

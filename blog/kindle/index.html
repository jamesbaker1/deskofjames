my kindle thinks i died in 2022

141 hours, 3,705 sessions, a 905-day flatline, and a resurrection: what seven years of kindle telemetry actually shows

amazon will hand you everything it knows about your kindle if you ask. i asked. it knows a lot: 410 csv files covering every reading session since 2019, every page flip, every dictionary lookup, every time i changed a font setting, every lockscreen ad it showed me, and, my favorite, every time the e-reader woke up and immediately went back to sleep.

i pointed seven analysis agents at the pile expecting a list of books. what came back was a three-act story told entirely in timestamps.

the flatline

reading minutes per month, aug 2019 → aug 2026. the shaded band is 905 consecutive days in which i did not open a book once.

act one: 2019, new kindle, 32 hours of reading, quantum physics and mountaineering disasters. act two: the slow fade. 25 hours in 2020, 19 in 2021, 90 minutes total in 2022. then may 4, 2022: the last session. after that, nothing. not a short gap. 905 days. all of 2023: zero minutes. as far as the telemetry is concerned i was deceased.

the library kept logging purchases through the silence, and the purchases explain it:

reading stopped in may 2022 and stayed stopped through law school and the startup that followed it. act three: a new kindle arrives april 7, 2026, at the start of a summer off, and within 24 hours i'm reading again. the four months since have produced 59 hours, more than any full year in the dataset.

the habit became structurally unbreakable

probability of reading tomorrow, given the current streak length. left: the 2019–2022 era. right: 2026. the red bars are certainties.

this is my favorite result in the whole export. in the first era, 57% of reading streaks died at day one; the chance a one-day streak survived to day two was 44%, and no streak ever passed 12 days. in 2026, once a streak reaches day 2, the observed probability that i read the next day is 100% through day 14 (70 of 70 day-observations continued). the only risky day is day 1. same brain, categorically different process: the longest streak is now 39 days, and i've read on 113 of the 124 days since the new kindle arrived.

the daily dose is boring in the best way: ~41 minutes per day in the first 30 days, ~41 in the last 30. what changed is the shape. median session grew from 15 seconds to 78, on fewer, longer sittings. the habit isn't growing; it consolidated.

midnight is my biggest reading hour

reading minutes by hour of day, in true local time (the jul–aug sessions are converted to sf/tokyo/taipei clocks per the phone's own timezone stamps). red bars are midnight through 4 a.m.

an honesty note first: i've been in asia since mid-july, and in the raw utc data that trip masquerades as a sudden 3 a.m. reading habit, then as me becoming a morning person. neither happened. corrected to where i actually was, my evening anchor is identical on both sides of the planet (center of mass ~7:30 p.m. local), and the night-owl finding survives cleanly: 26% of every minute i've ever read, about 37 hours, happened between midnight and 4 a.m. local time, with midnight itself the single biggest hour (924 minutes).

it's not insomnia, it's momentum: 86% of late-night sessions are continuations of an evening session that didn't stop. one detail worth noting: on the asia leg, the after-midnight share collapsed to 2%. jet lag did what intention never has.

the median session is 35 seconds

left: share of sessions by length. right: share of actual reading time. 86% of sessions are under five minutes; the 5–15 minute sittings carry the most minutes.

70% of all sessions are under two minutes, which initially reads as a destroyed attention span. it isn't, quite: 79% of those micro-sessions are warm-ups, followed by more reading within half an hour. a typical reading day is one main sitting that carries 74% of the day's minutes, plus a handful of short attempts around it.

median session length followed a u-curve: 147 seconds in 2019, down to 12 seconds in early 2026, back to 78 in the last 30 days. the longest continuous sitting of the entire seven years happened three weeks ago: 190 minutes of shackleton's endurance in a tokyo hotel.

what i actually read: three epochs

the content tells the same story as the timestamps, so the agents named my reading eras like geological periods:

  • the optimization eon (2019–2022), 4,666 min. gladwell, sinek, collins, "built to last", a gates biography, self-improvement, and quantum physics primers after midnight. fiction: 2.9%.
  • the founder flicker (2024–25), 268 min. five weeks of saas playbook, lean startup, and venture deals on a phone, then 14 minutes in all of 2025.
  • the survival & feeling supereon (2026), 3,552 min. the first week is attachment and trauma psychology (attached, the body keeps the score); then it finishes the jordan peterson book abandoned 1,434 days earlier, and moves through glass castle, shackleton, horror novels, caro. fiction jumps to 19.5%. four months out-read the best full year of the first era.

one theme survives every era: people enduring extreme circumstances. into thin air, on desperate ground, endurance, the glass castle: about 24 hours lifetime, the most durable interest in the record. physics did not survive; it fell from 524 minutes to 16 to 6 across the eras, and the book i've spent the most time on ever, 7.3 hours of what is real?, a book about the unfinished quest for the meaning of quantum physics, remains unfinished.

the "not interested" list is its own dataset: atomic habits, sapiens (rejected twice), outliers, blink, zero to one, infinite jest. per the recommendation logs, amazon's response to someone who rejected two gladwell books was to recommend more gladwell.

part two: the physics

everything above is narrative. the rest of this post drops the narrative and treats the habit as a physical system: the same 3,705 timestamped sessions, run through the machinery normally used for earthquakes, income distributions, clinical trials, and animal navigation. none of the models were told where the eras are.

the changepoint detector re-dates the death

bayesian online changepoint detection (adams & mackay 2007) watches the daily read/no-read sequence and reports when the underlying regime probably changed. it was given no dates. it found the resurrection on its own: strongest changepoint of the whole series at 2026-04-07, posterior 0.91, one day off the new kindle's arrival (a gaussian model on weekly minutes independently picks the same week at 0.98).

the surprise is the death. the last session was may 2022, and i started law school that fall, so the natural story is that classes killed the habit. the detector disagrees: it places the changepoint at late december 2021 (p≈0.5–0.7 across both models). the data backs it: november–december 2021 was an intense reading stretch (reading on 60% of days), and from december 28 onward the read-probability collapses to 0.011: seven scattered days in five months, then nothing. reading died eight months before orientation, during the application year. law school didn't kill the habit. it inherited a body.

the other boundary tells the same story in reverse. graduation (may 2025) did not revive anything: reading stayed dead through the startup year that followed. the detected resurrection is 2026-04-07, the day the new kindle arrived, three weeks before the startup wound down, at the start of a summer off. institutional boundaries don't move the needle in either direction; the hardware date does.

weekly reading minutes (bars) and the changepoint posterior (red, bernoulli model on daily read/no-read). the two dominant spikes are dec 2021 and apr 2026. neither date was supplied to the model.

reading is self-exciting: 75% of sessions are aftershocks

a hawkes process models events that trigger more events: each occurrence temporarily raises the rate of further occurrences, with the excitation decaying exponentially. it's the standard model for earthquake aftershocks. fitted to session start times by maximum likelihood (nine starts per fit, all converging to the same optimum):

eraμ, spontaneous starts/dayα, aftershock fractionexcitation half-life
2019–20220.300.7410.5 min
20263.110.766.3 min

the branching ratio α says ~75% of all sessions are aftershocks: triggered by a recent session rather than by a spontaneous decision to read. half the triggering force is gone within 6–10 minutes. and the parameter that separates the dead era from the current one is not the cascade mechanics, which barely moved. it's μ, the spontaneous rate, which increased 10×. the difference between not reading and reading 59 hours in four months is entirely in how often the first session of a burst happens. everything after that first session was always automatic.

fitted intensity λ(t) on 2026-05-06, the busiest day in the record (28 merged sessions, tick marks). the rate swings from a floor of 0.13 sessions/hour to a peak of 24.8, a 190× range. this is what a reading day looks like to the model: quiet, then chain reactions.

the gaps are scale-free

the time between sessions has no typical value. fitted against lognormal, exponential (what a memoryless poisson reader would produce), and stretched-exponential alternatives, the inter-session gap distribution is a power law in every era, and it isn't close: the exponential loses by thousands of AIC points. 2026 tail exponent: α = 1.44 across the full range from one minute to days.

log-log survival function of inter-session gaps, 2026 era. dots: the 1,564 observed gaps. solid: fitted power law. dashed: the exponential a poisson process would produce, which fails immediately. a straight line on these axes is the signature of scale-free behavior.

burstiness index (barabási's B, where 0 is poisson and +1 is maximally bursty): +0.48 in the first era, +0.55 in 2026, the same range as human email behavior. the texture of the habit never changed. what changed is that the bursts now happen every day.

book survival is memoryless, and paying doubles it

kaplan-meier survival curves over the 61 books with ≥10 minutes of reading, with "death" = abandonment and time measured in hours invested (completed and still-active books are censored):

probability a book is still alive after x hours of reading. black: all books. red: sideloaded documents. gray dashes: store-bought.

  • P(a book survives past 2 hours of investment) = 53%. median lifetime: 2.5 hours of reading.
  • the fitted weibull shape is k = 1.04. abandonment is memoryless. hours already invested buy a book no safety; the sunk-cost fallacy is apparently not one of my failure modes.
  • the one significant split: sideloaded books die at 2.0× the hazard of purchased ones (log-rank p = 0.017; median 1.7 h vs 3.4 h). i read sideloads more, but i abandon them faster. paying for a book roughly doubles its life expectancy.

attention inequality: gini 0.68

treat each of the 115 titles ever opened as a citizen and reading minutes as income. the resulting gini coefficient is 0.685, more unequal than income in south africa (0.63), the most unequal major economy. the top 10% of books absorb 47% of all reading time. across days the gini is 0.578.

lorenz curve of attention across books. the diagonal is perfect equality; the sag is how far reality deviates from it.

the circadian phase survived a 13-timezone move

session start times, mapped onto a 24-hour circle and fitted with von mises distributions, in true local time for wherever i was. the home leg of 2026 (new york, 1,015 sessions) versus the asia leg (tokyo/taipei, 500 sessions):

  • circular mean start time: 19:00 at home, 19:22 in asia. phase difference +21.5 minutes, 95% CI [−52, +96], statistically indistinguishable (watson-williams p = 0.36).
  • concentration went up in asia (κ = 1.18 vs 0.32): reading got tighter around the evening, not looser.
  • at home the distribution is bimodal: a broad afternoon-evening component plus a very sharp mode at 0:54 (κ = 16.5, nearly a fixed appointment). in tokyo the after-midnight mode vanished entirely.

fitted time-of-day densities on a 24-hour clock, 2026. black: home (ny). red: asia leg. the home curve's sharp lobe just past midnight is the 12:54 a.m. mode; the asia curve has none.

the phase held across half the planet. what the trip removed was only the after-midnight appointment, which is consistent with it being an artifact of the home schedule rather than of the clock.

monte carlo: what the rest of 2026 looks like

a generative model built from the 2026 era: the observed streak-hazard curve (laplace-smoothed, since several bins are at 100% and nothing deserves probability 1), daily minutes bootstrapped from the 113 observed reading days, starting from the actual aug 9 state (16-day streak, 59.2 h). 20,000 simulated year-ends:

  • total 2026 reading: median 126 hours, 80% interval [118, 136]. for scale, 2019 through 2025 combined was 82.
  • reading days in 2026: median 242 of 365.
  • P(the 39-day streak record falls by dec 31) = 12%.
  • P(a 100-day streak): 0 in 20,000 runs. the day-1 hazard is too high; the model says the streak always eventually resets at a skipped day, then rebuilds.

left: distribution of the longest streak achieved by dec 31 (the spike at 39 is "record stands"). right: distribution of total 2026 hours.

one caveat outranks the others. the model assumes april–august behavior persists, and it won't get the chance: i start a big-law job on september 8. the last two times a demanding institution entered the picture, the changepoint detector later found that reading had died months before the official start date. the 126-hour median is conditional on a world that ends in four weeks. if a future export shows a changepoint in the late summer of 2026, this post predicted it.

things the telemetry would rather i didn't know

  • buying the ad-supported kindle saved $20 and has cost 432 minutes of lockscreen ads (572 impressions). that's an effective wage of $2.78/hour for watching ads, below every us minimum wage. i have clicked exactly one ad, ever.
  • blended cost of my library: $20.61 per hour actually read. 79% of the dollars sit in books with under ten minutes of reading. best value: a $1 thriller i read for 3.2 hours ($0.31/hr). worst: influence, $22 for 4.2 minutes ($318/hr), and i own two editions of it.
  • amazon doesn't store search text, only lengths. but at 2:26 a.m. on jul 18, a 25-character search, the exact length of "the count of monte cristo", opened that book's page, and by the afternoon one of only two audible listens in seven years was logged: the count of monte cristo.
  • 276 of my 282 logged settings changes are toggling one setting: the reading-progress display, cycled between "time left in chapter", "time left in book", and "page number", 138 times in may 2026 alone. current answer: "time left in book".
  • on the phone, reading gets interrupted every 83 seconds (757 "app backgrounded" events against 17.6 hours of reading). the e-reader just idles to sleep every 12.5 minutes. that ratio is the entire phone-vs-e-reader argument in one number.
  • kindle points: 446 earned lifetime, 254 silently expired, 162 remaining. more points have expired than currently exist.
  • font settings across seven years and two devices: bookerly, size 4, justified. two recorded experiments with size 5, both reverted within minutes.
  • my "kindle" data export is 38% fire tv stick telemetry. the stick was used 5 days in 8 months, its screensaver out-ran netflix 118 minutes to 92, and on all 5 tv days my reading minutes were exactly zero.
  • one of the e-reader's internal settings is named, in production telemetry shipped to millions of devices, "prower saver".

what i take from it

the whole dataset reduces to one finding: my reading life is not a function of discipline, intention, or how much i claim to love books. it's a function of whether a dedicated e-ink device is physically near my bed, and whether something with the demands of law school is in session. when the device was there, i read 78 hours, then 59 more and counting, on a streak process that hasn't failed past day two. when it wasn't, eighteen months of phone-only reading produced four hours.

the $110 device outperformed the self-image by an order of magnitude. that seems worth knowing.

method notes

  • hawkes: exponential kernel, exact recursive log-likelihood, 9 random starts per fit; all converged to identical optima. events <1 min apart merged. subcritical in every fit.
  • changepoints: BOCPD with beta-bernoulli (daily read/no-read) and normal-gamma on sqrt(weekly minutes), hazard 1/250; reported changepoints are run-length posterior peaks.
  • survival: 61 books, 38 abandonments, 23 censored (12 completed, 11 active at export). log-rank tests; weibull/exponential hazards for the ratios.
  • circular: von mises MLE and 2-component mixtures by BIC; watson-williams for the phase test plus a 10,000-rep bootstrap CI. timezones corrected per the phone's own stamps: ny → sf (jul 15) → tokyo (jul 19) → taipei (jul 31).
  • monte carlo: 20,000 runs, add-1 smoothing on hazard bins; the 31+ day bin rests on only 10 observations, so the record probability is the least certain number here.
  • the map: coordinates are hand-assigned semantic placement, not an algorithmic embedding; era centroids and distances are computed from the placed points.

data: amazon "request my data" → kindle, exported aug 2026. 410 files; sessions from the reading-insights and device logs, cross-checked against whispersync, library index, and lockscreen-ad telemetry. timestamps converted from utc to true local time (nyc, then sf jul 15–19, then tokyo/taipei, per the phone's own timezone stamps). every chart is drawn from the real arrays, and every fitted parameter (hawkes, von mises, kaplan-meier, BOCPD, the 20,000 monte carlo runs) is embedded in this page's source.

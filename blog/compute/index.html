not your compute, not your model

open weights arrived. the machines to run them did not. on the arithmetic of local inference, the rest of the stack, and a club in new york.

the crypto world left behind exactly one saying that turned out to be durably true: not your keys, not your coins. every exchange collapse proved it over again, which is the only test a saying like that ever faces. i've been thinking about it lately with the nouns swapped, because i believe the same principle now applies to ai: not your compute, not your model.

open models are having a remarkable run, and yet the people they were meant for aren't really reaping the rewards. deepseek ships frontier-adjacent weights under an mit license, meta ships llama under terms you can live with, and all of it is sitting on hugging face tonight, free to download. the trouble is that almost nobody who downloads those weights can actually run them. i want to walk through why, and then tell you about a club in new york that's trying to do something about it.

the arithmetic

start with the sizes. deepseek v3.2 has 685 billion parameters and is fp8 native, which puts the weights alone at about 685gb before the kv cache takes a single byte. kimi k3, the largest open model right now, is 2.8 trillion parameters, closer to three terabytes. for comparison, the largest consumer gpu nvidia sells holds about 32gb. even if you quantize deepseek down to 4 bits, it still wants about 340gb, which works out to eleven consumer cards wired together. and you can't actually wire eleven consumer cards together, because the high-speed interconnect that makes multi-gpu inference work is the real product, and it isn't sold at the consumer tier.

so the license says the model is yours, but the memory map says it belongs to whoever owns an 8x h100 node. those start around $250,000 racked, or you can rent one by the hour from the same few clouds the open weights were supposed to make optional. the weights are open; the socket they need is closed.

weights are the binary

there's a second, quieter problem, which is that "open weights" and "open source" get used interchangeably and mean different things. calling weights open source is like calling a compiled binary open source. the source of a model is everything that produced it: the training data, the filtering pipeline, the training code, the schedule, the post-training recipe, and the evals that decided which checkpoint shipped. a typical open release includes none of those. what you get is the finished artifact and a license to run it, on hardware you don't have.

the rest of the stack

even with weights and hardware in hand, inference is its own software problem. you need an engine (paged kv cache, continuous batching, quantized kernels), a scheduler that decides whose job runs when the vram runs out, and a way to move jobs between machines without reading them. none of that arrives with the weights either. i find it clarifying to picture the stack under a running model as six layers, of which "weights" is only one:

  • training data
  • training code and recipe
  • weights
  • inference engine
  • scheduling and routing
  • power and cooling

the fight over layer three is the loud one, because it's the only layer with a license attached. the other five tend to get decided by default, and the default is renting. renting is genuinely fine, right up until the landlord deprecates the model you built on, or rate-limits you in the middle of an experiment, or logs what you send. i don't think the fix is everyone buying their own 8x h100 node. i think it's a cluster that a community owns and operates together, decentralized by design.

the club

which brings me to the new york compute club. members rack their own hardware somewhere in the five boroughs, and the club schedules jobs across all of it. jobs travel as libsodium sealed boxes, so the coordinator that routes them sees only ciphertext, a node id, and a self-reported wattage. it cannot read what you run. the threat model is written down, and it is honest about what the design doesn't protect against, which is how you can tell it's a real threat model.

the software is public and unfinished. nycc-engine is a small inference engine, with a paged kv cache, continuous batching, priority scheduling, and a numpy reference model you can read in an afternoon. nycc-grid is the mesh: sealed jobs, signed node traffic, and an untrusted coordinator kept small enough to audit. newyorkcomputeclub is the site, which renders manhattan as a load histogram. the load numbers are invented until enough nodes exist to report real ones, but the buildings are not: the lights above the island are 60 hudson st, 111 8th ave, and the teleport. pull requests move your application up the pile.

i should be honest about scale: the cluster is currently a few nodes and an extension cord. that is exactly what the applications are for, and they're open at newyorkcomputeclub.com/about.

there's an obvious next chapter, too: not your power, not your model. an h100 turns electricity into gradients at 700 watts a card, and whoever meters that electricity holds a veto over everything above it. one member has a basement in red hook and strong opinions about generators. but that's a later post.

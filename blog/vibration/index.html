vibration

does shaking people for a living damage their brains? probably not, but nobody has ever checked

i started from an idea that sounds reasonable out loud: years of riding motorcycles or operating heavy machinery deliver a lot of vibration to the skull, and maybe that accumulates the way repeated sub-concussive hits do in contact sport. millions of people drive, dig, and haul for a living. it seemed worth checking.

so i checked it before writing any analysis code. two things came back, pointing in opposite directions. the mechanism doesn't survive the arithmetic. and nobody has ever checked anyway.

revised august 2026. i asked at the top of the original version for someone to check the arithmetic. someone did, and a lot of it was wrong. the conclusion holds, but the margin was overstated on both channels, one of my simulation's two worlds wasn't the world i said it was, the job-exposure matrix statistics were in sample when i presented them as validation, and the evidence section left out every study i had myself graded as pointing toward harm. the corrections are inline below rather than in a footnote, marked as corrections, with the original numbers kept next to the new ones. the pattern in them is not flattering and is worth naming: every single error ran in the direction of my own conclusion.

the magnitude problem

the sub-concussive framing needs vehicle vibration to be doing something mechanically comparable to a head impact. it isn't close.

peak linear0.04 – 0.7 g ordinary driving

peak rotationalorder 10 – 40 rad/s²~1000 rad/s²
charactersustained, 4–10 Hztransient, high-jerk

on linear acceleration that is two orders of magnitude below sub-concussive thresholds in ordinary driving, closing to under one order on the shock-rich agricultural and mining machinery at the top of the range. on rotational, which is the quantity that actually drives diffuse axonal injury, it is one and a half to two orders below. there is no transient component.

an earlier version of this paragraph said the 4–10 Hz band is one the head and neck low-pass filter. that was wrong, and wrong in the direction that flatters the conclusion. seat-to-head transmissibility peaks at 3–10 Hz and is greater than one around 4 Hz, so the band vehicles actually excite is the band the body amplifies. filtering only starts above about 10 Hz. the magnitude gap is what carries the argument here, not any help from the anatomy.

a correction, august 2026. this table first read 0.04 – 0.7 g and ~1 rad/s², and claimed three orders of magnitude on rotational. both numbers were too flattering to my own conclusion. the linear figure came from crest factors of 1.41 – 3.96, but the measurement table in my own repo records a median crest factor of about 23 for agricultural machines, which is where the ~2.8 g worst case comes from. the ~1 rad/s² was worse: i had cited it to Paddan and Griffin, but it is the input level their shaker was set to, not a measurement of any vehicle. the nearest real field data i have since found is Cation et al. 2008 on forestry skidders, 0.33 – 0.79 rad/s² weighted rms at the seat, which converts to the order of 10 – 40 rad/s² peak.

no published paper makes this comparison directly. i assembled it from cited measurement inputs, so it's derived arithmetic rather than a quoted result. it's the most important number here and it deserves someone checking my work. that was not a rhetorical invitation: someone did check it, and the numbers above are what survived.

two caveats i should have carried over from the repo. first, this is a comparison of peak against peak, and the two exposures are nothing alike in duration: eight hours a day for thirty years integrates on the order of a billion loading cycles against roughly ten thousand for a decade of contact sport. which one does more damage depends on how steeply damage scales with amplitude, and the crossover sits around an exponent of five, where ISO 2631-1's own VDV metric uses four. so the argument is not pure arithmetic. it assumes brain injury is steeply threshold-like, which i think is right, since no threshold-free damage mechanism has been demonstrated for brain tissue at these strain levels, but it is a biological premise and it should be stated as one. second, none of this covers repeated-shock populations such as high-speed boat crews, where slams of 2 g and up carry exactly the transient character vehicle vibration lacks. the argument below is about trucks, tractors and motorcycles.

which means the hypothesis i started with, vibration acts as chronic sub-concussive trauma and damages the brain mechanically, is the wrong shape. if anything is happening it's indirect: vibration at 4–10 Hz reliably makes people drowsy within half an hour, and chronic sleep disruption does have a cognitive cost. that version is testable, and it makes a discriminating prediction: adjust for sleep and the association should mostly dissolve. mechanical injury wouldn't behave that way.

what the literature actually has

  • the largest review of acute after-effects found cognition inconsistent, with no pooled estimate. only postural stability degraded reliably. the review covers 32 studies and 114 exposure levels in total, but the cognitive arm of it is 11 studies and 52 trials, and i should not have quoted the larger number next to the cognitive null. it also covers after-effects only. a separate meta-analysis does exist for during-exposure performance (Conway 2007, 115 effect sizes) and it does find degradation, mostly visual and fine-motor.
  • the classic positive result was n=16 and a later study at n=54 was null. i previously called that a failed replication. it isn't one. the larger study varied vibration magnitude between subjects, at 18 per cell, where the original varied it within subjects, so on the contrast that produced the original result it is the less powerful design, and it was framed as a question about noise and vibration combined rather than as a replication.
  • the one study pairing vibration dosimetry with a neurodegenerative outcome found an inverse association with parkinson's, OR 0.67 (0.48–0.94), for ever-exposed. i left out the rest of that abstract, which i shouldn't have: higher exposure intensities had consistently elevated odds ratios, and the effect reached significance at intermediate intensities once exposure was restricted to the ten years before diagnosis. that restriction is the analysis designed to strip reverse causation, and it is where the harm signal shows up. see the note below on why i should have been more careful with this study than with any other.
  • vibration-as-exercise trials meta-analyse to a cognitive benefit, g = +0.375. i keep listing this and i should stop leaning on it. that literature is standing on a plate at 20–50 Hz for minutes a day, given to older or deconditioned people, and my own research notes say in writing that it has to be screened out as a trap for naive searching. it is an exercise intervention, not eight hours a day seated at 4–8 Hz.
  • chronic epidemiology: no cohort or case-control study has ever measured cognition or dementia as an endpoint in a chronically vibration-exposed population. i previously wrote "zero cohort studies, zero case-control studies, the literature is empty," which was too strong, and contradicted by the parkinson's study two bullets up, which is a case-control study of chronic occupational vibration with a central nervous system outcome.
  • the evidence i left out. my own repo grades several findings as pointing toward harm and none of them made it into this post, which was a real failure of even-handedness. the most occupationally realistic acute study (60 minutes at the EU limit value) found reaction time up 6% and vigilance lapses doubled, dose-dependently. a study at 3–7 Hz, the exact band i argue matters, found selective attention degraded at magnitudes at and just above the EU action value. in animals there is hippocampal blood-brain-barrier opening after 63 days of exposure but not after one, and one lab reporting neuronal apoptosis and cerebral edema in rats. i discount the animal work, and i think fairly: it is one lab running the same experiment twice, unreplicated for eleven years, at roughly eight times human dose, with no barrier or glial assays. but discounting it in the open is different from omitting it.

a note on that parkinson's study, because this is the sharpest thing wrong with the original version of this post. the whole methodological point i make below is that occupational designs manufacture spurious protective associations, because the people whose health is failing leave the job. i then took a protective association out of an occupational case-control study and used it as evidence that the prior should be low. i can't have it both ways. by my own argument that OR 0.67 is exactly what healthy-worker survivorship produces, and the latency-restricted strata are the more informative part of that paper.

i also excluded the entire hand-arm vibration literature on the grounds that it has "no established connection to central cognition." the dosimetric case for excluding it is fine, since hand tools and vehicle seats are not the same exposure. the reason i gave is false. there is a paper called Involvement of the central nervous system in vibration syndrome reporting delayed P300 potentials in vibration-exposed workers, with the delay largest in the rock-drilling group that carries the most whole-body exposure. that is objective electrophysiology with a cognitive endpoint and an internal exposure gradient, and it is the one occupational-vibration literature containing positive central findings. excluding it by a rule that happened to be worded wrongly is how you end up with a review that only points one way.

that combination is the whole reason to bother. the prior on direct mechanical harm should be near zero, and i should keep that qualifier attached, because the magnitude argument rules out one pathway rather than vibration neurotoxicity in general. vascular, barrier and inflammatory routes are untested rather than excluded. and a field with no chronic studies at all can't tell anyone in these jobs anything, so a precise null would be the first empirical statement on the question. i wrote the protocol so that finding nothing counts as success rather than as a wasted year.

why the obvious study fails, live

the tempting design is to compare cognition between people who drive for a living and people who don't. below is the data-generating process from the repo, running in your browser. it builds a population where i set the true effect of vibration exactly, then adds what actually happens in occupational cohorts: education sorts people into jobs, vibration arrives bundled with noise and exhaust and shift work, and people whose cognition is slipping stop driving.

a second correction. the version of this table i first published had the cross-sectional estimator at −0.0096 with a 10.5% false-positive rate, and i described it as biased. it wasn't. my "world where vibration does nothing" had a leftover parameter in it making vibration lower baseline cognition by 0.01 per unit of dose, so that estimator was being marked wrong for correctly recovering an effect i had forgotten to switch off. with the null world actually null it comes out at −0.0009 with a 5.5% false-positive rate, which is fine. the tell was there in my own output: both cross-sectional estimators returned identical numbers in the null world and the effect world, across all 200 replicates, which should have made me look.

the naive estimator reports a large protective effect of vibration in a world where vibration does nothing, in every single replicate. when a true harm of −0.02 is present it still reports +0.239: wrong sign, twelve times the magnitude. bias doesn't shrink with sample size, so no cohort is big enough to rescue it.

i'd bet that's behind any claim you've seen that professional drivers or motorcyclists have unusually sharp reaction times. it isn't a finding about vibration. it's a finding about who quits.

how much to believe this, stated plainly, because a simulation only ever tells you about the world you built. the +0.24 is a readout of one number i chose: a healthy-worker selection strength of 0.55, which appears nowhere in my research notes with a citation, and which the artefact scales linearly with. set it to zero and the artefact disappears. and the mechanism i describe in the paragraph above this one, people whose cognition is slipping stopping driving, turns out to contribute nothing: switching it off changes the answer by about 0.004. what the simulation actually shows is narrower than i implied. select a sample on its outcome, then regress that outcome on the selection indicator, and you recover the selection coefficient. that is a real and standard hazard, and it is worth demonstrating, but it is a mathematical identity rather than a discovery about occupational epidemiology, and the magnitude of it in any real cohort is unknown. by the same token my primary estimator's clean 5.5% is not evidence that it works. the generator contains no confounder that the estimator doesn't already adjust for, so it is unbiased by construction. that is a check that i wired the regression up correctly, nothing more.

measuring the exposure, badly

no cohort large enough to matter measures vibration directly, so it has to come from a job-exposure matrix. i built one from the public-domain O*NET work-context element for whole-body vibration (894 occupations) and calibrated it against jobs whose A(8) has actually been measured in the field. it doesn't work very well, and that's worth saying out loud:

the third correction, and the one that changes what this matrix is for. i originally published only the left-hand column, and it is entirely in sample: eight calibration anchors, fitting two parameters, then scored on the same eight points. the honest version is the right-hand column. a leave-one-out variance explained of −0.09 means that out of sample the calibration predicts measured vibration worse than simply guessing the average of the anchors. on magnitude, this matrix has no demonstrated validity at all.

two smaller things in the same direction. the "log-scale correlation 0.66" isn't an independent statistic, it is just the square root of the in-sample variance explained from the same two-parameter fit, and on eight points its confidence interval runs from about −0.09 to 0.93. and the ×1.90 was computed with the wrong degrees of freedom for a fitted model; done properly in sample it is ×2.00. the rank correlation of 0.74 is the one number here i'd still stand behind, because nothing is fitted to produce it, though on n=8 it is marginal at p = 0.04.

frequency of exposure isn't intensity of exposure. truck drivers rate 2.73 on the frequency scale and measure 0.45 m/s²; shuttle drivers rate 1.28 and measure 0.47. the worst failure is at the bottom, where the scale floors out: office clerks measure 0.068 m/s² and the calibration hands them 0.265. because that floor is structural, every one of the 894 occupations comes out at 0.261 m/s² or above, which is more than half the EU action value. floral designers and software managers included. a threshold count off this matrix is not meaningful.

i previously wrote that this makes a null "conservative in the wrong direction," on the reasoning that inflating the unexposed group's dose compresses the contrast and drags the slope toward zero. that reasoning is the guarantee for binary misclassification, and it does not carry over to a continuous mismeasured exposure, where compressing the assigned scale inflates the per-unit slope rather than shrinking it. worse, the error here has the wrong structure for that argument anyway: it is uncorrelated with the assigned value and correlated at 0.76 with the truth, which is the Berkson signature, and Berkson error does not attenuate. it leaves the coefficient roughly alone and destroys the precision instead. my own simulation codes the opposite kind of error, and at about a quarter of the magnitude the matrix actually has. so the dose–response test stays pre-specified on exposure quantiles, where the ranking is trustworthy, continuous dose stays secondary, and the real conclusion is that getting a magnitude-based matrix such as SweJEM is not an optimisation, it is a precondition.

two things that died on contact

the original plan had an indonesia arm: household motorcycle ownership crossed with community road quality, differenced around road improvements. IFLS has excellent cognitive data: two waves, 21,013 adults, within-person change computable. it has no motorcycle variable at any level. motorcycles are folded into one asset category with cars, boats and bicycles as a single rupiah figure, and no wave asks anyone how they travel. the design has no independent variable. the road arm fails separately: 17 of 310 communities went from unpaved to asphalt between 2007 and 2014, against 9 that went the other way, which is reporting noise rather than resurfacing.

uk biobank is scientifically the right cohort and currently unavailable: applications paused until late 2026 after a breach, affiliation mandatory, earliest data in hand Q1–Q2 2027. i don't have a university affiliation, so it's someone else's cohort. CHARLS replaces it as the starting point: about three days to access, free, and it carries household motorcycle and electric-bicycle variables directly.

the experiment i'd rather run

the review turned up a cheaper and far more decisive gap than any cohort analysis. nobody has measured head-level vibration during normal on-road riding or driving. every ground-vehicle measurement in the literature is taken at the seat. seat-to-head transmissibility is exactly the quantity the magnitude table at the top turns on, and for cars, trucks and motorcycles it is currently inferred rather than measured.

i originally wrote this as "nobody has ever measured head-level vibration during normal riding or driving," full stop, which is too strong. in-flight head and helmet accelerometry does exist for helicopters and carrier aircraft. it is worth knowing what it shows, because it does not help me: head vibration around 5.4 Hz came out amplified relative to the seat. that is consistent with the transmissibility correction further up, and it is a reason to want the ground-vehicle measurement rather than to assume the answer.

accelerometers at seat and head, across a few vehicle types and road surfaces, would settle the mechanistic question far more directly than analysing a hundred thousand job histories, and for a rounding error of the cost. if someone wants to actually answer this, that's the study.

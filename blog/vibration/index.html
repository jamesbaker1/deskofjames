vibration

does shaking people for a living damage their brains? probably not, but nobody has ever checked

i started from an idea that sounds reasonable out loud: years of riding motorcycles or operating heavy machinery deliver a lot of vibration to the skull, and maybe that accumulates the way repeated sub-concussive hits do in contact sport. millions of people drive, dig, and haul for a living. it seemed worth checking.

so i checked it before writing any analysis code. two things came back, pointing in opposite directions. the mechanism doesn't survive the arithmetic. and nobody has ever checked anyway.

the magnitude problem

the sub-concussive framing needs vehicle vibration to be doing something mechanically comparable to a head impact. it isn't close.

vehicle vibration at the headsub-concussive impact
peak linear0.04 – 0.7 g10 – 30 g
rotational~1 rad/s²~1000 rad/s²
charactersustained, 4–10 Hztransient, high-jerk

one and a half to two orders of magnitude below sub-concussive thresholds on linear acceleration, and about three orders below on rotational, which is the quantity that actually drives diffuse axonal injury. it sits in a band the head and neck low-pass filter, with no transient component at all.

no published paper makes this comparison directly. i assembled it from cited measurement inputs, so it's derived arithmetic rather than a quoted result. it's the most important number here and it deserves someone checking my work.

which means the hypothesis i started with, vibration acts as chronic sub-concussive trauma and damages the brain mechanically, is the wrong shape. if anything is happening it's indirect: vibration at 4–10 Hz reliably makes people drowsy within half an hour, and chronic sleep disruption does have a cognitive cost. that version is testable, and it makes a discriminating prediction: adjust for sleep and the association should mostly dissolve. mechanical injury wouldn't behave that way.

what the literature actually has

  • the largest review of acute effects (32 studies, 114 exposure levels) found cognitive after-effects inconsistent, with no pooled estimate. only postural stability degraded reliably.
  • the classic positive result was n=16. the larger replication at n=54 was null.
  • the one study pairing vibration dosimetry with a neurodegenerative outcome found an inverse association with parkinson's, OR 0.67 (0.48–0.94).
  • vibration-as-exercise trials meta-analyse to a cognitive benefit, g = +0.375.
  • chronic epidemiology: zero cohort studies, zero case-control studies. the literature is empty.

that combination is the whole reason to bother. the prior on a real effect should be near zero, but a field with no chronic studies at all can't tell anyone in these jobs anything, and a precise null would be the first empirical statement on the question. so i wrote the protocol so that finding nothing counts as success rather than as a wasted year.

why the obvious study fails, live

the tempting design is to compare cognition between people who drive for a living and people who don't. below is the data-generating process from the repo, running in your browser. it builds a population where i set the true effect of vibration exactly, then adds what actually happens in occupational cohorts: education sorts people into jobs, vibration arrives bundled with noise and exhaust and shift work, and people whose cognition is slipping stop driving.

drag horizontally to set the true effect, or pick one. each dot is one simulated study of 1,200 people; the heavy line is the running mean. the dashed line is the truth. set it to no effect and watch the naive estimator carry on reporting one.

the same thing, at full size

200 replicates, n=8,000, in a world where vibration does nothing:

the naive estimator reports a large protective effect of vibration in a world where vibration does nothing, in every single replicate. when a true harm of −0.02 is present it still reports +0.239: wrong sign, twelve times the magnitude. bias doesn't shrink with sample size, so no cohort is big enough to rescue it.

i'd bet that's behind any claim you've seen that professional drivers or motorcyclists have unusually sharp reaction times. it isn't a finding about vibration. it's a finding about who quits.

measuring the exposure, badly

no cohort large enough to matter measures vibration directly, so it has to come from a job-exposure matrix. i built one from the public-domain O*NET work-context element for whole-body vibration (894 occupations) and calibrated it against jobs whose A(8) has actually been measured in the field. it doesn't work very well, and that's worth saying out loud:

rank correlation with measured A(8)0.74
log-scale correlation0.66
typical prediction error×1.90

frequency of exposure isn't intensity of exposure. truck drivers rate 2.73 on the frequency scale and measure 0.45 m/s²; shuttle drivers rate 1.28 and measure 0.47. the worst failure is at the bottom, where the scale floors out: office clerks measure 0.068 m/s² and the calibration hands them 0.265, a 3.9× overestimate.

that one matters more than the scatter elsewhere. inflating the unexposed group's dose compresses the contrast and drags the dose–response slope toward zero, so a null from this matrix is conservative in the wrong direction, partly an artefact of the instrument rather than a fact about biology. so the dose–response test is pre-specified on exposure quantiles, where the ranking is trustworthy, and continuous dose drops to secondary.

two things that died on contact

the original plan had an indonesia arm: household motorcycle ownership crossed with community road quality, differenced around road improvements. IFLS has excellent cognitive data: two waves, 21,013 adults, within-person change computable. it has no motorcycle variable at any level. motorcycles are folded into one asset category with cars, boats and bicycles as a single rupiah figure, and no wave asks anyone how they travel. the design has no independent variable. the road arm fails separately: 17 of 310 communities went from unpaved to asphalt between 2007 and 2014, against 9 that went the other way, which is reporting noise rather than resurfacing.

uk biobank is scientifically the right cohort and currently unavailable: applications paused until late 2026 after a breach, affiliation mandatory, earliest data in hand Q1–Q2 2027. i don't have a university affiliation, so it's someone else's cohort. CHARLS replaces it as the starting point: about three days to access, free, and it carries household motorcycle and electric-bicycle variables directly.

the experiment i'd rather run

the review turned up a cheaper and far more decisive gap than any cohort analysis. nobody has ever measured head-level vibration during normal riding or driving. every measurement in the literature is taken at the seat. seat-to-head transmissibility is exactly the quantity the magnitude table at the top turns on, and right now it's inferred rather than measured.

accelerometers at seat and head, across a few vehicle types and road surfaces, would settle the mechanistic question far more directly than analysing a hundred thousand job histories, and for a rounding error of the cost. if someone wants to actually answer this, that's the study.
